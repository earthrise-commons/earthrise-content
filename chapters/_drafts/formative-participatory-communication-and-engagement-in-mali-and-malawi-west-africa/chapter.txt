Summary: <p>When my friend and former <em>National Geographic</em> colleague, Josh Korenblat, invited me to contribute a chapter on international sustainability work, I reflected on my formative experience two decades ago in Mali, West Africa, as well as the lessons learned over the years leading to the development of Cornell’s first Cross-campus Sustainability Leadership Program. My work and research in Sub-Saharan Africa and my efforts in educating the next generation have been through the lens of respectful communication and dialogue, intentional partnership, and community-led initiatives. In Mali, I learned the value of participatory methods, which place listening, inclusion, and collaboration at their core.</p>

Many conservation efforts today rely on compassion or outrage, which are limited sources of energy, or enforce compliance through fear. Instead, we need a&nbsp;pedagogical motivation that is expansive and renewable: wonder. Wonder guides us beyond narrow self-interest and complements reason with imagination. Korenblat’s learners stay in relationship with the more-than-human world (Abram) via nature journaling. Using pictures, words, and numbers, students inquire into the <em>umwelt</em> or worldview of other creatures. This pedagogy acknowledges that the biodiversity crisis is too vast to leave to experts. Educators can, instead, nurture an amateur understanding using inspirational&nbsp;models from Goethe’s <em>tender empiricism</em> and Antoine de Saint-Exupéry’s <em>The Little Prince</em>. Korenblat’s approach operates in the spirit of the original meaning of the word <em>amateur</em>: one who acts from innate love.
