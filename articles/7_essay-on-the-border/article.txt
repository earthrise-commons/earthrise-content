One day in a winter month, I walked two towns over and took a northbound bus across thirteen stations. Somewhere in the middle, I began to feel anonymous, acutely aware that no one knew who I was. C tells me that the first people deported across the border in 1865 were railway workers from <em>Guangdong </em>and<em> Fujian.</em> We muse on this fact, chewing sunflower seeds. <em>What people? How many?</em> He didn’t know. No one did.&nbsp;</p><p>I am as far from home as I’ve ever been. The desert is captivating, unfamiliar, like a nameless stranger on a plane. I see javelinas, bighorn sheep, scorpions of every color. Throughout the night, I hear the Border Wall bulldozer whir past with sharp, guttural sounds. In the morning, after we pack up our hammocks, there is never any trace of us left, but the upturned earth is greasy, like a corpse chewed by bugs.&nbsp;</p><p>The project to wall the border is strange to me, especially the extensive administrative conflict over 60 feet of desert space. That space is significant to no one and devoid of anything. Over the fence, Sonora looks identical to Arizona: dried, blistered, endless.&nbsp;</p><p>C tells me the names they call him in the Border Patrol.&nbsp;</p><p>“I am used to mopping up a pile of shit that the American makes,” he says, “because I am from Cabo.”&nbsp;</p><p>One day, we hike three hours to unload six gallons of water under a Red Cross flag. Migrants walk the steep rocky interstices of the desert, C says, instead of flat grasslands where they are easily spotted. Their flesh is strewn around each other, bulbous from the heat.&nbsp;</p><p>Since that day, the dead began revealing themselves to me, in shoes, drained water bottles, dirty clothes, trails of trash. In the desert, everything lives and dies anonymously. Most of the time, no corpse is found, and if there is a corpse, the Santa Cruz County Coroner’s Office writes: John Doe, Jane Doe. In the adobe house, there is a woman whose feet bleed so profusely we can see bone. She hurriedly leaves the encounter after we wrap her up, but when she walks away, she has already bled through her bandages.&nbsp;</p><p>One day during the monsoon season, I walk down to a river to wash my face and clothes. Later, I ask C what the name of the river is, to which he replies, what river? It existed only when I bathed in it. Call it whatever you want, C says. The encampment I work at is a space without names: volunteers don’t give or call each other by their names and are strictly prohibited from soliciting the name of anyone.&nbsp;</p><p>There were no names to those Chinese men, deported over the US-Mexico border in a country’s first act of exile. We know nothing about them, except that on Donner Pass, researchers exhumed an opium pipe, a few porcelain bowls.&nbsp;</p><p>I saw those bowls in a museum case. White porcelain, a pink peony blooming in the middle. They were a key to my memory, almost everywhere in my early years: my paternal grandmother eating with her back against the side of the bus. My maternal grandmother, crouching on the field with the bowl between her hands.&nbsp;</p><p>The San Andreas Valley is one of the deadliest crossings on the entire US-Mexico border. This is what C said to me, after I told him about my passion for the desert.&nbsp;</p><p>That is to say, the desert doesn’t care whether you’re passionate about it. Have I really felt thirst before? A bighorn sheep was following an ancestral trail to Quitobaquito Springs when it faced the interminable Border barrier. Its corpse was found in the concertina wire, mangled and dehydrated.&nbsp;</p><p>That is to say, I walked up to three gallons of water in a dry wash and found them half-slashed open, like the slasher couldn’t care enough to see it through. There is nothing human about the desert.&nbsp;</p><p>Wallace Stegner immortalizes the experience of the railway worker by describing <em>the Chinese</em> as not quite human…more objective, like a dog, or a donkey. When <em>they</em> died, they were sometimes registered as John Doe. Eight cattle, surrounding each other, rotting on the earth.&nbsp;</p><p>What is the right relationship to distance?&nbsp;</p><p>What is the right relationship to the dead?&nbsp;</p><p>I think it says a lot about me that I need to go to the antipode of the world to find out what is and is not home.&nbsp;</p><p>The desert does not care about names, it is not an intellectual project, it is a wildness iterating again and again, furious and passionate and meaningless.&nbsp;</p><p>I need to leave myself to see myself. And sometimes that means seeing myself not in human terms, but as the thing that wanders the terrible lonely borderline seeking water in the dark.&nbsp;</p><p>Somewhere in the sands of the desert, there is an animal, with a gaze blank and pitiless as the sun. The great poet Kahlil Gibran says this to a cow he is about to eat: by the same power that slays you, I too am slain; and I too shall be consumed. For the law that delivered you unto my hand shall too deliver me.&nbsp;</p><p>Names lie, humans lie, borders lie, but the desert has never been anything except for what it is. One night, a group of twenty migrants came in after midnight, made food for themselves, and all stayed in Trailer 5.&nbsp;</p><p>When we woke up in the morning, they were gone.
